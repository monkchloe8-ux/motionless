Why this exists

Web motion is usually treated as a style choice. It is also a physical one: the same techniques that make a site feel alive — parallax, autoplay, endless movement — can cause nausea, migraine and distraction for a large minority of people. This is a small experiment about that gap. Same content, four amounts of motion, a dial for each. You decide how much, and we publish what people keep.

How we got here

Motion has been part of the web's language for two decades — from the Flash era, through the parallax boom and “Snow Fall” scrollytelling, to today's WebGL spectacle. Each wave made the web more alive and, often, less usable. In 2019 browsers finally added a way for people to ask for less motion. Most sites still treat that as an afterthought.

The static version is designed

Turn any dial to zero and you don't get a broken page — you get a composed, still version of the same thing. The site respects your system's reduced-motion setting and starts calm if you ask it to. That's the point: accessible can be designed, not degraded.
